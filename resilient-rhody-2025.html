01 · Levers & strategies for adaptation

Two intervention layers stacked on top of each other: 79 statewide Actions (programmatic, policy, and financing moves) and 10 Solutions (physical, costed interventions on priority assets). Both sit inside a four-part typology of what resilience actually does to an asset.

The plan does not present a single, hierarchical strategy list. It layers three vocabularies. First, the four strategy mechanisms — Protect, Accommodate, Restore, Rethink — which name what a solution does to a hazard. Second, the 79 Actions, which are programmatic (build a program, update a code, stand up a database, fund a workforce). Third, the 10 Solutions, which are physical and site-specific, costed against priority assets. The Actions are the enabling layer; the Solutions are the built layer.

The four mechanism types p. 44 (Plan Summary)

Every solution strategy is tagged with one or more of these. In practice, the plan leans toward hybrids — a wetland restoration is Restore + Accommodate; retreat programming is Rethink + Restore.

Protect
Defend vulnerable areas using physical or engineered measures. E.g., shoreline protection, seawalls, flood barriers.
Accommodate
Adapt buildings and infrastructure to withstand changing conditions. E.g., elevation, retrofits, backup power.
Restore
Rehabilitate natural systems to buffer against hazards. E.g., green infrastructure, dune restoration, wetland reconnection.
Rethink
Reevaluate planning and support relocation from high-risk areas. E.g., managed retreat, TDR, zoning, ordinance reform.

The 79 Actions Ch. 2

Actions replace and expand the 61 actions from Resilient Rhody 2018. They are programmatic — funding needs of $121M–$304M cover programs, coordination, data, and workforce, not construction of the underlying capital projects. Construction dollars sit in the Solutions layer.

These five come up repeatedly in the executive summary, and each one has cross-references from multiple domain sections.

  • Action 4.02 — State Resilience Standards. A single umbrella standard across residential, commercial, roads, wastewater, stormwater, drinking water, electrical, and open space. This is the plan's "one code to rule them all" move.

02 · Prioritization approach

Two frameworks, sequenced. The Asset Prioritization Framework filters vulnerable assets into a priority list. The Project Prioritization Framework then ranks projects on those assets. Both are weighted composites, both are meant to be reusable by municipalities.

The prioritization work is the most methodologically-defended piece of the plan. It has its own chapter (Ch. 8), a 200-asset shortlist derived from an initial 10,000-asset pool, and stakeholder-derived weights. The two frameworks answer different questions.

Where the framework hedgesThe plan's own qualifications on prioritization

The plan opens the prioritization chapter with an unusually candid disclaimer:

"Prioritization within complex, interdependent systems is inherently challenging. There is no single definitive method for ranking assets, as infrastructure, communities, and ecological systems function as an interconnected network. No asset operates in isolation, and any prioritization approach necessarily simplifies the dynamic relationships that influence statewide resilience. This complexity is most evident in the State's natural systems, which underpin the State's built, community, and cultural infrastructure."

It then adds a load-bearing sentence that is easy to miss: "Accordingly, this Plan affirms that protecting, managing, and restoring natural systems is not ancillary to resilience efforts but central to them, even if not explicitly highlighted among priority assets." This is the plan telling you the priority list understates natural systems and you should read it accordingly.

The RIIB pivot — grants to layered financing

The evolutionWhy the state moved beyond grant-only support

The plan lays out an evolution the state has already run through, and asks you to fund the next step:

Phase 1 — Grants only. The Municipal Resilience Fund started as pure grant capital, coupled with a planning process (MRP) that gave under-resourced municipalities the capacity to identify projects. Equitable but capped.

Phase 2 — Revolving loans. RIIB's Resilient Rhody Infrastructure Fund (RRIF) leverages its state-revolving-fund experience to offer low-cost, long-term loans. Every $1 of public capital supports roughly $3 in project cost.

Phase 3 — Layered financing (current ask). RIIB can now stack revolving loan capital, state bond proceeds, green bonds, and federal infrastructure funds behind the same project. The result is described as "a layered sustainable financing model that does not rely on a single funding source" — and importantly, one that "enables small cities and towns to punch above their fiscal weight."

Reading the plan against your working thesis

Insurance access is the resilience currency the plan doesn't quite name.

The plan touches insurance in three places. Action 19.05 tracks climate impacts on Rhode Island insurance markets and calls for strategies on rising costs, data sharing, and building code updates. Best Practice #2 imports parametric insurance and NFIP affordability precedents (Fremont, Utah, CCRIF, NYC). The non-traditional funding menu lists parametric insurance and insurance premium discounts as capital tools.

What the plan does not do is name insurance access — not premium reduction — as the outcome variable. The value cascade the plan hints at but leaves unarticulated: carrier exit → uninsured properties → mortgage market disruption → tax base erosion → municipal fiscal stress. Framing resilience investment as the mechanism that keeps homes and infrastructure insurable is a stronger argument than framing it as premium relief, because premium relief is not robust to soft-market conditions.

Where this matters: pitch conversations with municipal finance leadership, RIIB, CoBank-adjacent rural finance, and any state that is pricing bond issuance against future insurability. This is a downstream product opportunity the plan structurally leaves open.

05 · Risk assessment & cascading effects

A five-hazard, four-time-horizon, two-scenario matrix applied to 21 asset types across 130,000 assets. The methodology is rigorous. Cascading effects appear, but as narrative — not as a second- or third-order modeling framework.

The Statewide Climate Vulnerability Assessment (Ch. 3) is the plan's technical backbone and its longest chapter — 266 pages. It uses the standard Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability = Risk equation, with vulnerability incorporating asset condition, age, and Social Vulnerability Index scores. This is not novel methodology, but the scale — 130,000 assets — is unusual for a state plan.

Emissions scenariosSSP2-4.5 vs SSP5-8.5 — what each one presumes

SSP2-4.5 · Intermediate. Carbon dioxide emissions stay near current levels through 2050, then decline but do not reach net-zero by 2100. Global mean surface warming lands roughly 2.7°C above pre-industrial by 2100 (IPCC AR6 central estimate). The plan uses this as its "policy-in-motion" case — meaningful mitigation happens, but not enough to hit the Paris ceiling.

SSP5-8.5 · Very High. Continued growth in fossil-fuel emissions throughout the 21st century; emissions increase substantially by 2100. Warming lands roughly 4.4°C above pre-industrial (IPCC AR6). The plan uses this as its stress-test case — most headline "% of assets at risk by 2100" numbers in the Plan Summary sit under SSP5-8.5.

Reading tip. The plan reports both, but the risk maps in Chapter 3 and the risk-bar totals in this tab default to SSP5-8.5 at 2100 unless otherwise noted. This is the upper bound the plan wants readers to weight. It also means: the "18% of assets at high/very high risk to coastal flooding by 2100" figure is the very-high-emissions figure, not the intermediate.

Time horizonsWhat each of the four horizons buys you
  • Present-day — baseline. Uses observed hazard data (FEMA FIRM for riverine, URI STORMTOOLS coastal for present-day surge). This is where the "dams already at high risk" and "10% of critical infrastructure at high riverine flood risk" numbers sit.
  • 2035 — near-term operational horizon. Roughly the useful-life period of capital already in the ground. Divergence between SSP2 and SSP5 is small here.
  • 2050 — mid-century. This is where scenario paths meaningfully diverge and where the plan tests whether currently-planned interventions hold.
  • 2100 — the stress case. Sea level rise assumption of 7 ft (used in the coastal risk maps under high-SLR). Rainfall intensity assumption of >10 inches during a 24-hour 100-year event across all counties by 2100 (SSP5-8.5). Wind speed assumption reaching 150 mph in Newport and Washington counties.

The plan does not model beyond 2100. Any product or project with intended life beyond century-end is outside the frame the plan gives you.

Data sourcesWhich downscaled dataset drives each hazard
  • Coastal flooding — URI STORMTOOLS coastal model, with 7-ft SLR overlay for late-century high-SLR scenarios. STORMTOOLS is the plan's most-cited data layer and its own recommended zoning input (see Action 14.13 and Best Practice #1).
  • Stormwater flooding — Northeast Regional Climate Center (NRCC) precipitation projections, downscaled via NASA GDDP (Global Daily Downscaled Projections). The plan uses 24-hour 100-year storm as its design storm.
  • Riverine flooding — FEMA Flood Insurance Rate Maps (FIRM) for present-day; not projected forward, so present-day risk is the primary readout.
  • Extreme heat — NASA GDDP + Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI). The plan counts "days per year above 90°F" as its primary heat metric.
  • Extreme wind — ASCE 7 hazard tool + downscaled projections for hurricane wind speeds. 150 mph is the plan's 2100 upper-bound assumption for Newport and Washington counties.
Cost estimate accuracyClass-5 bands: +100% / -50%

Every solution cost estimate in this plan is Class 5 per the AACE International cost estimate classification matrix. That carries a formal accuracy band of +100% / -50%. In practice: a solution shown as "$3.5M – $15M per mile" for road adaptation could realistically run $1.75M on the low side or $30M on the high side once site-specific work is done.

Cost basis: RSMeans 2025 City Cost Indices for Rhode Island, escalated to 2025 dollars via the Engineering News Record Construction Cost Index. Benchmarks are Arup internal database + desktop research; the plan is candid that "costs for actual projects will vary based on defined extent of scope, project-specific risks, site constraints, environmental scope, construction delivery method, stakeholder engagement, funding mechanisms, existing asset operations, and market factors."

Reading tip: when comparing two solution cost estimates in the plan, remember they carry identical uncertainty bands. Do not treat differences smaller than 2× as meaningfully separating.

On cascading (second- and third-order) effects

You asked whether the plan handles 2nd- and 3rd-order impacts. Answer: it names them, and structurally rewards them, but does not model them.

How cascades enter the planThe three places where second-order thinking shows up

1. In the prioritization framework. Criterion N3 (Criticality) is explicitly about cascades: "Disruption would trigger widespread, cascading impacts across multiple systems and communities, extending well beyond the immediate area." Assets that produce cascade risk get a higher priority score. This is the plan's most concrete mechanism for including cascading effects.

2. In narrative "Consequence of Inaction" statements per solution. Each Solution's funding-strategy page carries a "Consequence of Inaction" scaled Low / Medium / High, and the narrative names cascades — e.g., transmission line failure → prolonged outages → hospital capacity loss → life safety. But these are qualitative, not modeled.

3. Per-hazard narrative callouts. Stormwater flooding → sewer overflows → contamination spread by riverine flooding is a two-step chain the plan describes. Extreme wind → downed lines → outages during a heat wave → hospitalization risk is another. Each is treated as a story, not a compound-risk calculation.

What the plan does not do: it does not run a quantitative cascade model — no fragility-curve chains, no interdependency graph, no explicit third-order (e.g., financial market or population displacement) impacts. The Statewide Climate Vulnerability Assessment uses fragility curves for individual asset risk, but not for system-of-systems cascade.

Implication for your reading: the plan is stronger on direct risk than on cascade risk. If you were pitching a resilience OS or an executive digital twin, the cascade-modeling layer is exactly the gap the state framework leaves open.

Named cascade chainsSpecific two-step chains the plan calls out
  • Coastal flooding → contaminant transport → life safety. Floodwater debris/contaminant load is repeatedly named.
  • Stormwater flooding → sewer overflows (CSOs/SSOs) → public health / water quality. Explicitly the plan's stormwater-plus-riverine framing.
  • Extreme heat → grid stress → outages → cooling capacity loss. Cited as "combination is particularly concerning."
  • Extreme wind → transmission line failure → transportation disruption → emergency access failure.Treated as the plan's most-cascade-heavy hazard chain.
  • Riverine flooding + damaged coastal wetlands → adjacent development more exposed to next storm. A rare instance where degradation of one asset changes the risk profile of another asset.
  • Heat → bacterial growth in drinking water systems → public health. Under-appreciated chain, called out explicitly.

While this assessment and Plan concentrated on identifying specific assets at risk from climate impacts, resilience efforts will require integrated solutions that address multiple asset types and operate across regional boundaries.— Plan Summary, key take-aways

This exact sentence appears twice — once in the front-matter Assumptions and once in the Conclusion. That kind of duplication in a 639-page document is not accidental. 08 · What's absent from the plan

Reading a plan is partly reading what it doesn't do. These are gaps I'd flag if the question were "what's a downstream product opportunity, or a critique to have in the room."

Gap · Cascade modeling

No quantitative interdependency model

The plan names cascade chains (grid failure → cooling loss → hospitalization) but does not model them. Criterion N3 (Criticality) scores potential cascades qualitatively per asset — there is no system-of-systems fragility curve, no interdependency graph, no compound-event simulation. If you were pitching a resilience OS, an executive digital twin, or CoBank-style rural interdependency analytics, this is where you'd plug in.

Gap · Insurance access framing

Insurance shows up as a funding mechanism, not as a resilience currency

Best Practice #2 covers parametric insurance and NFIP affordability, and Action 19.05 tracks climate impact on RI insurance markets. But the plan does not frame maintaining insurability as the outcome variable that ties resilience investment to household financial survival. The value cascade — carrier exit → uninsured properties → mortgage market disruption → tax base erosion — is not named. This is the framing you've been holding for Systemiq's US work; it's genuinely not in the plan.

Gap · Population & economic geography

Retreat is treated as a policy option, not a demographic reality

Managed retreat and voluntary buyouts appear as line items (Action 11.03, Best Practice #10). What is not modeled: where displaced populations go, what happens to the receiving communities, how the municipal tax base shifts, or what the housing-market spillover looks like. The plan is missing an internal-migration or municipal-fiscal-impact layer.

Gap · Private-sector counterparty risk

Utility, insurer, and lender exposure sit outside the frame

Rhode Island Energy (the electric utility) shows up as a partner in Action 6.02-.04 and DPUC/PUC/OER are named. But the plan does not evaluate private-sector counterparty risk — what happens if RIE's cost recovery on transmission hardening drives ISR rate cases, or if reinsurance withdrawal cascades to primary carriers. The private-side transmission risk is under-treated relative to its magnitude.

Gap · Federal-uncertainty scenarios

The federal-funding hedge is stated, not modeled

The plan repeats the "changing federal priorities" caveat twice, verbatim, and pitches the 1% state-budget model as an alternative. But there is no scenario laid out for what happens if BRIC / FMA / PROTECT / SNEP are meaningfully cut or restructured. The $121M–$304M Actions estimate and the Solution-level construction dollars all lean on federal matching in the assumed cases. A downside scenario is not shown.

Gap · Health outcomes as a first-class KPI

Health equity is a framing, not a measured outcome

Health Equity Zones drive engagement and priority-asset selection, and Community Health & Resilience is the largest sub-bucket (10 Actions). But the plan does not commit to a health-outcomes tracking layer — hospitalization from heat, respiratory admissions during air-quality events, mental health impacts from displacement. Action 18.06 assesses these risks; nothing here tracks whether investments move the numbers.

Gap · Historic resources at the plan's edges

Historic assets are mentioned but not scoped

Historic Resources is listed as an asset type on p. 24 but does not appear in the Priority Assets list, and does not get its own Solution. Best Practice #7 references Newport's historic-building elevation guidelines, but the plan itself doesn't scope Newport's or Providence's historic districts as at-risk assets. In a state where the historic economy is a real revenue lever, that's a hole.

09 · Reading guide to the source

If you're going to actually open the PDF, here's the routing. The document is nine chapters plus a plan summary. Some chapters are essential; some are reference.

Read first (if you have 30 min)

  • Plan Summary (pp. 9–53) — everything above, in ~45 pages. Foreword, purpose, engagement, vulnerabilities, actions, solutions, funding.
  • Chapter 8: Prioritization Framework (pp. 619–621) — the full criteria tables. Three pages.
  • Chapter 9: Best Practices (pp. 622–638) — the "steal-this-idea" chapter.

Read next (if you have 2 hrs)

  • Chapter 5: Solutions (pp. 474–526) — the physical interventions with cost estimates.
  • Chapter 6: Future Investment Strategy (pp. 527–586) — the funding readout by entity, asset type, and state benchmark.
  • Chapter 4: Priority Assets (pp. 448–472) — the shortlist that everything else runs against.

Reference layer

  • Chapter 2: Actions (pp. 77–181) — all 79 Actions, one per page. Use as a lookup, not a read-through.
  • Chapter 3: Vulnerability Assessment (pp. 182–447) — the technical bulk. Read a specific asset-hazard pair when you need it.
  • Chapter 7: Funding & Financing Inventory (pp. 587–618) — mechanism catalog. Reference when scoping a specific project.

Skim, don't read

  • Chapter 1: Community Engagement Summary (pp. 54–76) — mostly the same content as the Plan Summary engagement section, more granular.
  • Assumptions & Qualifications sections — same text repeats verbatim across the document. Read once.
